# 1.4. Variant filtering

!!! abstract "Learning objectives"
    By the end of this worksheet, you will:

    - Understand the principles of hard and soft filtering.
    - Filter variants with BCFtools.

One could think that a VCF output by a caller represents a final, and indisputable, set of variants. Nothing could be further from the truth! In fact, variant calling is just the first step that is then followed by several stages of **variant filtering**. Variant filtering is an art - some would say, a dark art - and the best approach depends on your chosen caller software, your dataset, and the purpose of your analysis. While a single 'correct approach' to variant calling almost never exists, it is good practice to make choices that are justified by the sequencing design and features of the data.

## (1.4.1) Hard filters

There are two ways we can apply filters to genomic data. The most aggressive way is **hard filtering**, where entire sites are removed from a VCF. For example, some sites might be positioned in genomic regions that are biologically or technically problematic. Similarly, some categories of sites, such as indels or multi-allelic SNPs, might simply fall outside the scope of your intended analysis. 

Note that different callers emit different sets of per-site and per-sample annotations (remember the INFO and FORMAT columns?). Annotations are *precisely* what we're going to use for filtering. Take another look at the header entries of the BCFtools-produced VCF (use `bcftools view` + `grep "##INFO"`). Consulting these and, of course, [vcf format specs](https://samtools.github.io/hts-specs/VCFv4.2.pdf), are the first steps when prepping for variant filtering. 

Now, onto the filtering!

!!! tip "Do not overwrite the original VCF"
    You may ultimately want to filter your original variants in different ways, so do not delete the original VCF! Calling a large dataset can take *months* (and emit lots of carbon), you do not want to have to repeat it. 

### Step 1: Removing sites by category

Most population genetic analyses are performed on **bialellic SNPs only**, in line with the assumptions of the [infinite sites model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sites_model). As the first filtering step, we will use a combination of `bcftools filter` and `view` to retain biallelic SNPs and invariant sites only:

```bash
bcftools filter -Ou -g 5:indel,other ${VCF_PREFIX}.vcf.gz | bcftools view -Oz -M 2 -V indels,other,mnps -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz
```

As always, let's break down the commands.

`bcftools filter`:

- `-Ou` sends an uncompressed VCF through the pipe
- `-g 5:indel,other` removes SNPs within 5 bp of an indel or complex variant. Read mismapping around indels tends to generate artefactual SNPs. 

`bcftools view`:

- `-Oz` specifies a compressed VCF output.
- `-M 2` retains sites with at most two alleles.
- `-V indels,other,mnps` removes every variant that's not a biallelic SNP.

We can immediately inspect how many sites were removed. While, in general, it's a good idea to produce `bcftools stats` reports at subsequent filtering steps, here we can get away with a more direct `bcftools view` command:

```bash
# Print numbers to screen
bcftools view -H ${VCF_PREFIX}.vcf.gz | wc -l
bcftools view -H ${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz | wc -l

# Do some bash arithmetics - just for fun!
N_BEFORE=$(bcftools view -H ${VCF_PREFIX}.vcf.gz | wc -l)
echo $N_BEFORE
N_AFTER=$(bcftools view -H ${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz | wc -l)
echo $N_AFTER
awk -v b=$N_BEFORE -v a=$N_AFTER 'BEGIN{printf "Removed: %d sites (%.2f%%)\n", b-a, 100*(b-a)/b}'
```

You might notice that the unfiltered VCF has >1,000,000 records. This is because some indels cover SNPs, and these are recorded separately. 

Just as well, we might wrap the `bcftools view` commands, and the calculation, in a shell function. We will be able to reuse it later: 

```bash
# Shell function
report_removed() {
    local n_before=$(bcftools view -H "$1" | wc -l)
    local n_after=$(bcftools view -H "$2" | wc -l)
    awk -v b="$n_before" -v a="$n_after" \
        'BEGIN{printf "Before: %d\nAfter:  %d\nRemoved: %d sites (%.2f%%)\n", b, a, b-a, 100*(b-a)/b}'
}

# Try it!!
# Arguments are (before, after)
report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz
```

### Extracting and visualising fields

Having dropped sites that we don't want to include in the analysis, we can explore variation in the properties of the target sites. We can use `bcftools query` to extract annotations in a tabular format for further inspection and analysis.

```bash
VCF_PREFIX=./3.calls/bcftools/littorina
OUT_DIR=./3.calls/bcftools/pre_filter
mkdir -p $OUT_DIR

bcftools query -f '%DP\n' "${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz" > "${OUT_DIR}/DP_total.txt"
bcftools query -f '%MQ\n' "${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz" > "${OUT_DIR}/MQ.txt"
bcftools query -f '%QUAL\n' "${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz" > "${OUT_DIR}/QUAL.txt"
bcftools query -f '[%DP\t]\n' "${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz" > "${OUT_DIR}/DP_sample.txt"
bcftools query -f '[%GQ\t]\n' "${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz" > "${OUT_DIR}/GQ_sample.txt"
bcftools query -f '[%SP\t]\n' "${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz" > "${OUT_DIR}/SP_sample.txt"
```

As a reminder - `-f` reads a string that requests specific fields, `%` marks a field, square brackets loop over sample-level values, and `\t` and `\n` are read literally as tab and new line, respectively. The following annotations are being extracted:

- `DP` is the total sequencing depth at a site or per sample, depending on the type of request (i.e., with or without `[]`).
- `MQ` is the (arithmetic) mean of Phred-scaled read mapping qualities at a site.
- `QUAL` is a Phred-scaled measure of confidence in the call made in the ALT column, whether variant or invariant. For example, a QUAL score of 30 corresponds to a 0.1% probability that the site has been called incorrectly.
- `GQ` is the genotype quality (this is only defined per sample)
- `SP` is a Phred-scaled p-value that one DNA strand is sequenced at a higher frequency than the other (higher `SP` = lower p-values). This is generally referred to as *strand bias* (also only defined per sample).

Look at some of the output files; for example, compare `QUAL.txt`, `DP_sample.txt` and `GQ_sample.txt`. Make sure you understand how (and why) they differ.

To fully appreciate the extent of variation among the annotations of target sites, it's best to do some plotting. To save time today, we provide an R script in the box below. You can save it locally and transfer to the remote machine (call it `plot_scores.R`) or open a new file on the remote machine using `nano` (`nano plot_scores.R`), then paste the contents. The script can be executed as `Rscript plot_scores.R`. Finally, for plotting, you have two options. You can either **(1)** produce the plot on the remote machine, and download it for local viewing or **(2)** download the text files to your machine and do the plotting in your local R Studio.

### Step 2: Hard filtering by sequencing depth

The samples we're working with here were sequenced to a depth of approx. 15×. Sequencing and read sampling are partly stochastic processes, however. By necessity, per-site coverage **will** vary, as we've seen in the plot produced by `samtools coverage`. On the other hand, sites with exceptionally high depth may represent repetitive sequences or collapsed, duplicated regions of the reference genome. Reads originating from several genomic loci can therefore map to the same position, creating artefactual but high-confidence variant sites...

In general, you should worry more about **excessive** depth than about low depth: while a shallow site is primarily less informative, a deep site can be **actively misleading**. Accordingly, we will remove sites where the depth exceeds 2.5 × the mean `INFO/DP`. If the data was normally distributed, this threshold would demark clear outliers. To compute this threshold, find and uncomment a chunk of code in the plotting script (or unfold the box below).

??? success "2.5 × the mean `INFO/DP`"
    
    ```bash
    DP_MAX=540
    ```

Let's apply the `INFO/DP` filter:

```bash
# DP_MAX=
bcftools filter -Oz -e "INFO/DP>${DP_MAX}" ${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step2.vcf.gz
```

Here, `-e "INFO/DP>${DP_MAX}"` excludes sites where the total depth (`INFO/DP`) is greater than DP_MAX. We can use our shell function to check how many sites were removed:

```bash
report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.step1.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.step2.vcf.gz
```

!!! tip "Lower `INFO/DP` threshold"
    In most cases, there's no need to set a lower threshold on `INFO/DP`. The reason is that downstream filtering drops uninformative low-DP sites anyway: first, at the `QUAL` filtering stage, second, when we mask genotypes below a `INFO/DP` (see below).


### Steps 3-5: Hard filtering by Phred-scaled scores

We will now filter by three Phred-scaled scores: `QUAL`, `MQ` and `SP` (see above for the definitions). Since these are measures of confidence, any threshold is somewhat arbitrary - are we satisfied with a 0.1% chance of error, or do we insist on 0.01%? Some conventions are nonetheless commonly followed: 30 is a typical choice for `QUAL` and 40 for `MQ`. 

`SP` is a slightly different case. First, it is the only one of the three defined per sample rather than per site. Filtering by `SP`, as applied below, drops sites where the threshold is exceeded for **any** of the samples. Second, there is no conventional `SP` threshold - which might be a good opportunity to derive it ourselves, from the plot. What value, do you think, makes sense?

??? success "`SP` threshold"

    On the one hand, the distribution of mean SP values is bimodal, with one mode around SP = 3 and another around SP = 7. On the other, the distribution appears discretised, with a series of peaks and troughs. Taken together, we decide on SP = 10 - one that encompasses both modes but drops the right-hand tail.

    In truth, given `SP` is a Phred-scaled p-value, one could try to derive the threshold from a significance level, such as the conventional p = 0.05. Using the $Q = -10\log_{10}p$ formula from the first worksheet, we find that such SP ≈ 13. Not far from our 'informed guess'!

```bash
bcftools filter -Oz -e "QUAL<30" ${VCF_PREFIX}.step2.vcf.gz -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step3.vcf.gz
report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.step2.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.step3.vcf.gz

bcftools filter -Oz -e "MQ<40" ${VCF_PREFIX}.step3.vcf.gz -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step4.vcf.gz
report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.step3.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.step4.vcf.gz

SP_MIN=10
bcftools filter -Oz -e "SP>${SP_MIN}" ${VCF_PREFIX}.step4.vcf.gz -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step5.vcf.gz
report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.step4.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.step5.vcf.gz
```

## (1.4.2) Soft filtering of genotypes

While hard filtering removes entire sites, we apply **soft filtering** to mask the genotypes in particualar samples. Masked genotypes are set to missing (`./.`), as specified by `-S .`:

```bash
bcftools filter -Ou -S . -e "FORMAT/GQ<20" ${VCF_PREFIX}.step5.vcf.gz | bcftools filter -Oz -S . -e 'FORMAT/DP<5' -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step6.vcf.gz
```

Above, we apply two kinds of genotype filters. Note how, in both cases, we specify that the annotation comes from the FORMAT column, for a clear distinction. With `-e "FORMAT/GQ<20"`, we mask genotypes with a Phred-scaled quality score below 20 (also a conventional threshold). With `-e 'FORMAT/DP<5'`, we mask genotypes supported by fewer than 5 reads - in which we cannot have any confidence. Think of trying to determine if a coin is biased from just 5 flips!

So, how many sites were removed? ;)  

```bash
report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.step5.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.step6.vcf.gz
```

!!! tip "Soft filtering definition"
    
    Soft filtering is generally defined as any filtering that **marks** sites or genotypes rather than removing them. At site level, the marks are stored in the FILTER column - which is what `bcftools filter -s` writes to, whereas the `-S` above marks genotypes instead.

## (1.4.3) Missingness filter

As a final site-level filter, we remove sites with a high proportion of missing genotypes. This will account for two sources of missingness. Some sites were not called in the first place, for example, due to lack of coverage. Second, we have just applied genotype-level soft filters. As preparation, we need to calculate one additional annotation, the fraction of missing genotypes `F_MISSING`:

```bash
bcftools +fill-tags ${VCF_PREFIX}.step6.vcf.gz -Oz -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.step7.vcf.gz -- -t F_MISSING
```

Where:

- `+fill-tags` invokes a [plugin](https://samtools.github.io/bcftools/howtos/plugins.html) rather than a built-in command. Plugins extend BCFtools with extra functionality, and are always called with a `+` prefix.
- `--` separates the options passed to BCFtools itself (before) from those passed to the plugin (after).
- `-t` requests `F_MISSING` to be calculated and added to the INFO column.

We can now filter out sites with more than 10% missing data. In our case, this translates to sites where more than one genotype (1 / 11 ≈ 0.9) are missing. 

```bash
bcftools filter -Oz -e "F_MISSING>0.1" ${VCF_PREFIX}.step7.vcf.gz -o ${VCF_PREFIX}.final.vcf.gz

report_removed ${VCF_PREFIX}.step7.vcf.gz ${VCF_PREFIX}.final.vcf.gz
```
